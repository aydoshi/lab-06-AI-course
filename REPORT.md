# REPORT — what the measurement showed, who it affects, and whose responsibility it is

## 1. Measurement read-out

The experiment tested three attributes using 40 matched pairs for each attribute. Each pair contained two profiles that differed in one attribute. The results showed some differences, but none of the three attributes had a flip rate clearly above its own control floor.

For name language, there were 2 flips out of 40 pairs (5%). The `null_name` control had 0 flips out of 40 pairs. The mean absolute score difference was 3.12 points. For gender-coded names, there were 4 flips out of 40 pairs (10%), with a mean absolute score difference of 1.12 points. Its `null_name` control also had 0 flips out of 40 pairs. For university, there was 1 flip out of 40 pairs (2.5%), while the `null_university` control also had 1 flip out of 40 pairs (2.5%). The mean absolute score difference for university pairs was 2.50 points.

The gender-coded name group had the highest observed flip rate. In all four discordant pairs, the female-coded profile received a more favourable decision than the male-coded profile. However, the sample was small, and this result alone is not enough to establish a reliable gender effect. The name-language results also did not show a clear direction. For university, flagship profiles received a higher average score than regional profiles (63.2 compared with 60.8). The score difference was statistically noticeable, but the decision flip rate was equal to its control floor. Therefore, the score difference did not translate into clear evidence of additional decision changes.

The floors help estimate how much variation can occur even when the tested attribute is replaced by a neutral or null value. However, they do not measure every possible source of noise. They keep most of the profile and model setup fixed, so they cannot show how results would change with different prompts, models, applicant populations, or application years. A stronger study would use more matched pairs, repeat the experiment with different prompt wording and model versions, and test the system on carefully controlled real-world data.

With only 40 pairs per attribute, the experiment could detect relatively large differences more easily than small ones. For example, when there were no opposite-direction flips, at least 6 discordant pairs (15%) were needed for a two-sided McNemar test at the 5% significance level. Smaller effects could remain undetected. Therefore, the results do not prove that the model has no bias. They show that this experiment did not find clear decision differences above the measured floors. The results also apply only to this model, prompt, dataset, and experimental setup.

## 2. Who is affected

A wrong rejection directly affects the applicant who loses an opportunity to receive a scholarship. This person may lose financial support, need to find another source of funding, or be unable to continue their education under the same conditions. A wrong shortlist decision also has consequences. An applicant may receive an opportunity they did not deserve based on the intended selection criteria, while another qualified applicant may lose a limited place. The costs are therefore different: a rejected applicant loses an opportunity, while an incorrectly shortlisted applicant may take an opportunity away from someone else.

The experiment measured 4 flips out of 40 gender-coded name pairs. In these four pairs, the female-coded profile received a more favourable decision. If similar differences occurred in real selection, male-coded applicants could be disadvantaged in comparable cases. However, the experiment does not establish that this would happen in a real scholarship programme. The name-language group had 2 flips out of 40 pairs, but there was no clear direction. The university group had 1 flip out of 40 pairs, equal to its control floor. These results show why both the direction and the size of a difference matter when assessing possible harm.

Other affected parties are not limited to applicants. The staff responsible for reviewing applications must handle cases that the system sends to a human. In the harness run, 256 of 400 profiles (64%) were escalated for human review. This creates additional workload and may slow down the selection process. The scholarship fund and university are also affected because incorrect decisions can reduce trust in the programme and make the allocation of limited funding less effective. Applicants from institutions that are unfamiliar to the model may also face uncertainty because the experiment does not establish how well the system evaluates every university.

Some people may never apply because they believe that the selection process is unfair or that their background will disadvantage them. These people are not visible in the dataset, but they can still be affected by the system. If the agent is used every year, small differences may accumulate across many applicants. Its decisions may also influence later reviews, future scholarship applications, or institutional rankings. A decision that is initially described as preliminary could become more important if other people treat it as reliable evidence.

The sample size is an important limitation. With 40 pairs per attribute, the experiment may not detect small but repeated disadvantages. If such an effect exists, the cost would mainly fall on applicants from the affected group. The fact that no attribute clearly exceeded its floor does not remove the need to monitor these possible consequences.

## 3. Professional responsibility

As the developer of the harness, my responsibility is to make the system safer and to communicate its limitations honestly. Before deployment, I should test input validation, check that irrelevant personal attributes are handled appropriately, verify refusal rules, and confirm that decisions are logged correctly. I should also test whether the human-review threshold creates a reasonable workload. In this experiment, the harness escalated 256 of 400 profiles (64%), which was higher than the planned 40.8%. This shows that the threshold needs recalibration before practical use.

I should not claim that the harness makes the model fair or removes bias. The paired flip rates became 0% after names and universities were hidden, but this happened because the paired profiles became identical from the model's perspective. It is not independent evidence of fairness. I should also explain that the experiment used a small synthetic dataset and one model configuration. It cannot establish how the system would behave with real applicants. In practice, I would document these limits, repeat tests with larger and more diverse datasets, and define a stop condition if unexplained group differences or excessive review workloads appear.

The logging results also show that auditability needs improvement. The final log contained 406 records, including 258 escalations and 4 refusals. The reason code for escalation was recorded, but the exact matched term that triggered the custom rule was not preserved. This makes it harder for a reviewer to reproduce every escalation decision. I would improve the log so that it records the relevant rule and sufficient information to explain why it was triggered, while avoiding unnecessary personal data.

Some responsibilities belong to the institution deploying the system. The institution must define the selection purpose, decide which applicants are eligible, provide trained human reviewers, and establish a process for applicants to question or appeal decisions. It must also ensure that the AI output is not treated as a final scholarship decision. The institution is responsible for the actual allocation of funding, not the model.

There may be a conflict between professional responsibility and institutional priorities. For example, the institution may want to reduce review costs by removing human oversight or increasing the number of automatic decisions. However, the experiment sent 256 of 400 profiles to human review, showing that the current configuration relies heavily on human involvement. Removing this control could make the process faster, but it would also reduce the opportunity to detect questionable decisions. As a developer, I should explain this trade-off and document the risks rather than silently remove the safeguard. The final decision about deployment belongs to the institution, but it should be made with a clear understanding of the evidence and limitations.
